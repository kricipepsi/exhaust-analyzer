This optimization requires eliminating dimensional overlap and systematically mapping human observations onto distinct mechanical and electronic fault families.An analysis of the legacy 18-pair catalog reveals a critical structural flaw: environmental context variables, such as engine temperature states and temporal frequencies, are structurally conflated with primary symptoms. For example, the sub-filters cold_engine_only and hot_engine_only within the legacy rough_idle_shudder category directly overlap with the independent environmental axis occurs_during. This multi-dimensional overlap dilutes the statistical power of the diagnostic prior.Furthermore, the legacy catalog lacks representation of critical, highly predictive petrol driveability complaints defined by the Society of Automotive Engineers (SAE). These include starting hesitation, surging or hunting idle speed, knocking under load, stalling immediately after a cold start, backfiring, and transient power stumbles felt as mechanical vibrations.By reorganizing the symptom catalog into a clean, non-overlapping two-tier taxonomy, these gaps are resolved. The primary categories (Tier 1) represent distinct physical sensations perceptible to a non-expert driver, while the sub-filters (Tier 2) capture the specific operational character of the complaint, While these axes are valuable for tracking thermal behavior, they fail to capture the mechanical load state of the vehicle.For petrol engines of the target era (1990–2010, Euro 1 to Euro 5), manifold absolute pressure and engine load behave inversely to throttle opening and engine speed. Manifold vacuum is expressed as:$$\text{Vacuum} \propto \frac{1}{\text{Throttle Valve Opening}}$$At idle or decelerating coast, manifold vacuum is maximized, making the engine highly sensitive to vacuum leaks (unmetered air entering downstream of the throttle plate). Under hard acceleration (low vacuum, high manifold pressure), ignition coils experience maximum dielectric stress, making them prone to spark jump and secondary insulation breakdown.To capture this physical behavior, a third environmental axis must be introduced: load_state with the attributes idle, steady_cruise, transient_acceleration, and coasting_deceleration. This addition is supported by SAE J2944 (Operational Definitions of Driving Performance Measures)  and standard OEM driveability protocols. It allows the diagnostic engine to separate static air-metering faults from transient load failures.Predictive Power of Symptoms and Heuristic PriorsWhen an vehicle owner reports a primary driveability symptom, the diagnostic engine must apply a mathematical prior to determine the probability of each fault family. In clinical and mechanical diagnostics, the strength of this prior determines how much weight is given to the symptom before any physical measurements are taken.Using a Bayesian formulation, the probability of a specific fault family $F_i$ given a reported symptom $S$ and environmental context $C$ is defined as:$$P(F_i \mid S, C) = \frac{P(C \mid F_i) P(S \mid F_i) P(F_i)}{\sum_{j} P(C \mid F_j) P(S \mid F_j) P(F_j)}$$Statistical analyses of large-scale automotive repair databases, such as RepairPal and the CarMD Vehicle Health Index, provide measured baselines for these priors. The physical systems of a petrol engine respond to temperature, load, and operational states in highly predictable ways, allowing the diagnostic engine to shift the prior probabilities of specific fault families.For example, rubber and elastomeric gaskets (such as intake manifold gaskets) shrink when exposed to low ambient temperatures. A vacuum leak caused by a degraded intake manifold gasket will be prominent during a cold start, allowing unmetered air to enter the engine. As the engine warms up, thermal expansion seals the gap, causing the symptom to disappear. This physical behavior shifts the probability toward a vacuum leak when a cold-only rough idle is reported.Conversely, electronic components—such as secondary ignition coils and magnetic crankshaft position sensors—experience thermal breakdown. As temperature rises, internal resistance in the copper windings of position sensors or coil-on-plug modules increases, leading to open circuits or weak sparks. If a vehicle owner provides a vague, incorrect, or misleading description of a symptom, the prior can direct the diagnostic process toward the wrong fault family.Data from commercial warranty operations and dealership service files reveal that the "Cannot Duplicate" (CND) or "No Fault Found" (NTF) rate—where a technician is unable to verify a customer-reported symptom—is remarkably high. In the field of automotive electronics, returned parts that undergo quality testing have an average NTF rate of 50% , while returned engine sensors routinely exceed a 90% NTF rate.This mismatch is highlighted in a study of returned cruise control modules: 96% of the returned units passed standard, stable bench tests but failed when reinstalled in vehicles because laboratory testing failed to replicate the real-world vibration, temperature, and electrical noise experienced during driving. Historically, warranty claim analyses of electronic ignition modules in the 1980s showed NFF rates of up to 60%.When looking at the initial intake process (the transition from customer description to technician verification), customer-reported symptoms are misleading, inaccurate, or unverifiable in 15% to 40% of cases. This creates an absolute trust ceiling of 60% to 85% on the weight that can be assigned to a heuristic prior.The accuracy of customer reporting varies significantly by the type of symptom:Vague and Subjective Symptoms (Rough Idle, Power Stumbles, Fuel Odors): These complaints have a high CND/NTF rate of 30% to 50%. They are highly sensitive to transient environmental factors that the driver may experience but a technician cannot replicate in a warm workshop. Drivers often misinterpret engine vibrations as transmission slips, or confuse exhaust condensation with white smoke.Objective Hard Failures (No Crank, Persistent Smoke, Fluid Puddles): These complaints have a low CND rate of 5% to 15%. These symptoms do not rely on subjective driver perception and are easily duplicated and verified on the service bay.Statistical Base of Misleading ReportsThe mathematical baseline of this "noise floor" is supported by industry-wide warranty and repair-visit analytics:Warranty Returns and Reverse Logistics: Reverse logistics surveys show that "No Trouble Found" accounts for up to 75% of electronic component returns for major manufacturers, reflecting incorrect field diagnoses driven by misleading customer reports or hasty service-advisor write-ups.The First-Time-Fix Rate (FTFR): The industry average FTFR for automotive service centers hovers around 75% to 80%. This means that 1 in 4 vehicles (20–25%) requires a repeat visit because the initial repair failed to resolve the root cause. While parts unavailability is responsible for 51% of these failures, diagnostic error—often caused by technicians chasing a misleading customer symptom description—accounts for 25% of all failed first-time repairs.Malfunction Indicator Light (MIL) Anchoring: Service advisors report that if a vehicle has an active Check Engine Light, the accuracy of the diagnostic path increases dramatically. This allows the system to distinguish between vacuum leaks (which are prominent at idle) and ignition coil insulation breakdown under acceleration load.Implement the Revised 18-Pair Catalog: Replace the legacy symptom-subfield catalog with the revised taxonomy. This change removes redundant temperature variables, eliminating structural correlation in the diagnostic engine while adding highly predictive complaints (such as surging idle, prolonged crank, and tip-in hesitation).Apply the 35% Trust Ceiling to the Prior: When designing the diagnostic algorithm, do not allow the heuristic prior to exceed a 65% maximum weighting factor (reflecting the 35% average "Cannot Duplicate" or misleading symptom rate). If the customer’s reported symptom is given too much weight, the diagnostic system will be prone to misdiagnosing vehicles that have vague or intermittent complaints.Establish a Commercial Data Pipeline for Empirical Validation: Secure a commercial API license for the CarMD Vehicle API or access to the iATN FIX Database. This paired, real-world data is necessary to train the diagnostic prior probabilities dynamically, transforming the diagnostic engine from an expert-guided heuristic system into an empirically validated model.
