# Feasibility of High Accuracy on a Small Partly Synthetic Automotive Fault Corpus

## Executive summary

On the evidence, a corpus of roughly 250–280 cases with about 12 classes, severe imbalance, partly synthetic or hand-filled values, ambiguous sentinel codes, hand-curated placeholders, and at least one low-separability evidence slice is **much more likely to be the binding constraint than the model** at a top-1 plateau near 50%. The biggest reasons are structural: noisy or uncertain labels reduce the measurable ceiling; ambiguous fabricated or imputed features can create spurious separability; minority classes with only 3–5 cases cannot support stable recall estimates; and when one evidence state maps to many fault families, the local irreducible error may already be high. In comparable noisy-label benchmarks, human label noise of about 9% to 40% materially reduced accuracy, and real-world noisy labels were harder than synthetic noise at the same nominal rate. In small-sample validation studies, 75–100 independent test cases are often needed just to estimate a good classifier’s performance with reasonable precision. citeturn25view2turn25view3turn41view0turn15search0turn29search2turn29search3turn19search11turn8search0

**Highest-leverage data action:** before more model work, create a **measured-real, provenance-audited evaluation subset** with explicit flags for observed vs imputed vs synthetic vs sentinel-coded fields, and re-audit labels on that subset. If the model still stalls there, the problem is genuinely separability-limited; if it does not, the current corpus was the bottleneck. citeturn6search19turn40search1turn40search19turn13search4turn12search4

## Label-noise impact on the ceiling

**Finding.** If a fraction \(r\) of evaluation labels are wrong and those errors cannot be recovered from the features, then agreement with the observed corpus is capped at about \(1-r\). As a practical ceiling, that means roughly **10% label noise implies an approximately 90% maximum achievable agreement with the corpus**, **15% implies about 85%**, **20% implies about 80%**, **30% implies about 70%**, and **40% implies about 60%**. This is a mathematical consequence of corrupted targets, not a benchmark average. Under class-conditional noise, the optimal decision rule itself shifts unless the noise process is explicitly modeled or corrected. citeturn41view0

Empirically, human annotation noise produces large losses. In the **CIFAR-10N** benchmark, standard cross-entropy reached **92.92%** on clean labels, **87.77%** on the **9.03%**-noise aggregate human label set, and **77.69%** on the **40.21%**-noise “worst” human label set. In **CIFAR-100N**, standard cross-entropy was **76.70%** on clean labels and **55.50%** on the **40.20%** human-noise set. The same paper also reported that human noisy labels were harder than synthetic noise at similar nominal rates: on CIFAR-10, standard cross-entropy was **77.69%** on human “worst” noise versus **86.69%** on synthetic noise of the same benchmark setup. That is directly relevant here because expert-curated automotive labels are more likely to be **instance-dependent** and ambiguous than clean class-conditional flips. citeturn25view2turn25view3

For estimation, **confident learning / Cleanlab** is one of the strongest practical methods. In synthetic CIFAR-10 settings with **20%–40%** label noise, confident-learning variants estimated the noise structure accurately in the **moderate-noise regime** and recovered many mislabeled examples with substantial precision and recall, though performance varied by method and sparsity assumptions. The original paper explicitly notes that estimation is accurate for **20%–40%** noise and reports label-error detection precision/recall in roughly the **mid-60s to 90s** depending on the variant and difficulty. That makes it useful for auditing a corpus like yours, but not a guarantee of perfect correction. citeturn26view0turn26view1

Independent relabeling is still essential. Inter-rater agreement statistics such as **Cohen’s kappa** or **Fleiss’ kappa** can quantify label reproducibility, but they are sensitive to prevalence and imbalance; in rare or dominant classes, kappa can look artificially low or paradoxical even when raw agreement is high. In your setting, report **raw agreement, classwise confusion, and kappa together**, not kappa alone. citeturn16search0turn17search0turn17search14

**Evidence and sources.** The theoretical basis comes from random and class-conditional label-noise learning theory, especially Natarajan et al. on risk minimization under noisy labels. Real-world noisy-label behavior comes from the CIFAR-10N / CIFAR-100N benchmark of human annotation errors. Noise-rate estimation and label-error localization come from confident learning. Agreement measurement comes from the inter-rater reliability literature, with caveats from the kappa-paradox literature. citeturn41view0turn24view0turn25view2turn26view0turn16search0turn17search0

**Confidence.** **High** for the general conclusion that mislabeled ground truth lowers the measurable ceiling; **medium** for any exact ceiling in your automotive corpus, because the exact cap depends on whether mislabels are random, class-conditional, or concentrated in ambiguous slices. citeturn41view0turn24view0

**Data gaps and domain-transfer caveats.** There is no peer-reviewed automotive study giving a universal “X% label noise → Y% top-1 ceiling” law for 12-way petrol fault-family classification. The cleanest quantified evidence comes from general ML benchmarks, not from vehicle diagnosis. The transfer is still strong because the mechanism is generic: corrupted targets reduce achievable agreement and distort learned boundaries. citeturn24view0turn41view0

## Synthetic, hand-filled, and sentinel values

**Finding.** Deterministic fill-ins such as **median imputation, manual fabrications, or hand-entered placeholders** can materially bias results because they **underestimate variance**, **shrink uncertainty**, and can **distort correlations and downstream coefficients or decision boundaries**. Reviews of single imputation methods explicitly warn that mean and median imputation ignore relationships with other variables and understate variability; classic missing-data texts further note that treating imputed values as if they were observed leads to underestimated standard errors and overconfident inference. In predictive settings, missing indicators can sometimes help, but simulation work shows they can also be **harmful**, particularly when missingness is related to the outcome or differs between development and deployment. citeturn29search2turn29search3turn29search22turn28search2turn28search11

The specific risk in your corpus is worse than ordinary missingness because some fields are not merely missing; they are **synthetic, hand-filled, or represented by ambiguous sentinel/limit values** such as **±127** or **999** that could mean “real saturated reading” or “no data.” Official data-cleaning guidance treats such codes as a special danger precisely because software or analysts may accidentally treat them as valid numeric measurements, skewing distributions and learned thresholds. Survey and official-statistics guidance recommends converting such codes into explicit machine-missing values or separate reason-coded missings, and preserving the provenance as a distinct flag. citeturn40search1turn40search19turn40search8

The standard practice here is not just “impute better.” It is to **make provenance observable**. Every field that is not directly observed should carry a companion flag such as **observed / derived / imputed / synthetic / manually overridden / sentinel / out-of-range / saturated**. Data-quality frameworks in health and observational data organize checks around **conformance, completeness, and plausibility**, which transfers well to automotive diagnostics: confirm that values are legal for the sensor, internally consistent with related channels, and distinguishable from coding artifacts. citeturn6search19turn40search5turn40search22

For your particular sentinel problem, the minimum defensible pattern is: retain the **raw code**, a **parsed value** if one exists, and an explicit **is-sentinel / reason-for-missing** flag. If **999** or **127** can be both a real physical limit and a missing-data code, that ambiguity is itself a data-quality failure; do not collapse them into one numeric variable during training or evaluation. That last recommendation is a synthesis from missing-data and data-validation standards, not a directly measured automotive threshold. citeturn40search1turn40search19turn6search19

**Evidence and sources.** The bias risks come from missing-data methodology and prediction-model deployment studies. The sentinel-code handling guidance comes from official data-cleaning and survey-data standards. The conformance/completeness/plausibility framing comes from established data-quality assessment work. citeturn29search2turn29search3turn28search2turn6search19turn40search1turn40search19

**Confidence.** **High** that unflagged deterministic fill-ins and ambiguous sentinel values can bias model development and validation; **medium** on the precise size of the bias in your corpus, because that depends on how often those values occur and whether they fall in highly discriminative fields. citeturn29search2turn28search2

**Data gaps and domain-transfer caveats.** I did not find a peer-reviewed automotive paper specifically quantifying the effect of ambiguous codes like **±127** and **999** inside a fault classifier for petrol passenger cars. The evidence is transferred from general missing-data, health-data, and official-statistics validation literature, where the underlying failure mode is the same. citeturn6search19turn40search1turn29search2

## Class separability and irreducible signal

**Finding.** The standard way to ask whether a dataset can support high accuracy is to estimate **how much irreducible error is already present in the data**, before discussing model capacity. The core toolbox is well established: **feature-overlap measures, neighborhood overlap measures, class-conditional density overlap, mutual information or conditional entropy, nearest-neighbor error bounds, and Bayes-error estimators based on graph statistics such as the Henze–Penrose divergence**. Data-complexity surveys group these measures into overlap, separability, and geometry/topology families. citeturn7search1turn7search8turn8search0turn8search17turn8search5

For imbalanced problems, overlap is often more harmful than imbalance itself. In a dedicated study of imbalanced data, class overlap degraded sensitivity, balanced accuracy, and AUC more consistently than class imbalance, and imbalance mattered most when overlap was already present. That directly matches your situation, where the “single most common evidence state” reportedly maps to about **seven fault families**. citeturn19search11turn19search23

In information-theoretic terms, if observed features carry too little information about the class label, **Fano-style bounds** imply a non-trivial lower bound on classification error. In practical terms: if the same evidence profile repeatedly appears under many fault families, the conditional entropy \(H(Y \mid X)\) is high in that region, and no classifier can reliably force those cases apart without additional evidence. For your specific slice, if that common evidence state is close to evenly shared across seven families, then the **local** top-1 ceiling for that slice would be only about **1/7, or 14%**, absent extra information. That calculation is an inference from your description, not a measured result. citeturn7search3turn7search11

Irreducible error is usually estimated in practice by one or more of four methods. First, estimate a **Bayes error rate lower or upper bound** from the sample using nearest-neighbor or graph-based estimators. Second, compute **overlap and neighborhood disagreement** measures class by class to locate collision regions. Third, measure **mutual information / conditional entropy** between candidate features and labels. Fourth, where possible, examine **near-duplicate or repeated cases with conflicting labels/outcomes**; those expose irreducible uncertainty or label inconsistency directly. No single estimator is definitive on a small tabular dataset, so triangulation matters. citeturn8search0turn8search5turn8search17turn7search1turn7search8

**Evidence and sources.** The methodological evidence comes from the data-complexity literature, Bayes-error estimation work, and information-theoretic classification bounds. The conclusion that overlap can dominate imbalance comes from the imbalanced-learning literature. citeturn7search1turn7search8turn8search0turn8search17turn19search11turn19search23

**Confidence.** **High** that your described many-to-one evidence state is a red flag for low separability; **medium** for any numeric Bayes-error estimate until the corpus is actually audited and measured, because these estimators get noisy in small multiclass samples. citeturn8search0turn8search17

**Data gaps and domain-transfer caveats.** I did not find a published automotive benchmark that estimates Bayes error for a 12-way petrol-fault table exactly like yours. The methods are imported from general ML and imbalanced classification, but the transfer is strong because they are explicitly designed to quantify intrinsic class difficulty independent of model choice. citeturn7search8turn8search0

## Synthetic vs real data and the sim-to-real gap

**Finding.** The literature strongly supports the claim that a synthetic or simulated diagnostic corpus must be validated against real data before its reported accuracy can be trusted. In an analogous **UAV fault-diagnosis** setting, one sim-to-real study reported that a classifier trained on simulated data achieved only **52.9%** accuracy on real-flight fault data, while a redesigned architecture plus domain adaptation raised that to **99.1%**. Another UAV study reported real-flight accuracy increasing from about **73%** for a baseline DCNN and about **80%** for improved features to **96%** after adding domain adaptation. These are not automotive engine datasets, but they are close analogues in **fault diagnosis under unsafe-to-collect real faults**, and they show that the sim-to-real penalty can be enormous. citeturn39view0turn38view0

Automotive-adjacent evidence from autonomous driving points the same way. In an automotive object-detection study, synthetic data plus only **10% real data** could outperform full-real training for the **person** class in some conditions, and fine-tuning from synthetic to real improved results over mixed training alone. The same paper concluded that **diversity and domain match mattered more than photorealism**. That is not the same task as engine fault diagnosis, but it supports a narrow, useful transfer: synthetic samples can help only when they cover missing regions of the real task and are validated against real distributions. citeturn34view0turn35view2turn36view1

Validation methods are standard. A partly synthetic corpus should be checked with **real-only holdout evaluation**, **classifier two-sample tests / adversarial validation** that try to distinguish synthetic from real cases, and **kernel two-sample tests** such as **MMD** to test whether the feature distributions differ. If a held-out classifier can distinguish synthetic from real substantially better than chance, or if real-only accuracy is materially lower than synthetic or mixed validation accuracy, your synthetic distribution is not yet trustworthy as a proxy for deployment. citeturn13search4turn12search4turn13search2

Automotive dataset practice also supports caution. **EngineFaultDB**, for example, is a much larger controlled laboratory dataset collected under simulated operational and fault scenarios on a specific spark-ignition engine; and a recent OBD-II review emphasizes the breadth of data-centric vehicular ML while real-world diagnostic generalization remains an active challenge. That is very different from a tiny mixed-origin ground-truth corpus intended to stand in for real maintenance cases. citeturn20search2turn20search6turn18search5

**Evidence and sources.** The strongest direct quantitative gap numbers came from UAV sim-to-real fault-diagnosis studies. Automotive transfer evidence came from autonomous driving and controlled engine datasets. Validation methodology came from two-sample testing and distribution-comparison literature. citeturn39view0turn38view0turn34view0turn13search4turn12search4

**Confidence.** **Medium** for the size of the sim-to-real gap in your exact domain, because direct petrol-passenger-car multiclass fault-family evidence is sparse; **high** for the recommendation that synthetic data must be validated on real-only holdout and distribution-shift tests before reported accuracy is believed. citeturn39view0turn13search4turn12search4

**Data gaps and domain-transfer caveats.** I did **not** find a peer-reviewed study reporting the exact synthetic-to-real accuracy gap for a small tabular OBD/exhaust/fault-code classifier covering 1990–2010 petrol passenger cars. Most tighter evidence is off-domain but still within fault diagnosis or automotive perception. citeturn39view0turn34view0turn20search2

## Sample size and class imbalance

**Finding.** With about **260** cases total, overall accuracy can be estimated only coarsely, and minority-family performance is effectively **unverifiable** at counts of **3–5**. Using standard binomial confidence intervals, an observed overall accuracy of **50%** on **260** cases corresponds to a roughly **44%–56%** 95% interval; even an observed **85%** would still imply only about **80%–89%**. So the overall point estimate is not meaningless, but it is far too blunt to certify family-level diagnostic quality. These interval widths are calculations from standard Wilson / exact binomial methods. citeturn14search4turn14search10

The per-family problem is much worse. If a family has **5** test cases and the observed recall is **3/5 = 60%**, the exact 95% interval is about **15%–95%**. If it is **5/5**, the interval is still only about **48%–100%**. With **3** cases, even **2/3** corresponds to about **9%–99%**. In other words, single-digit class counts do not support claims like “this family is reliably diagnosed.” They support only anecdotal impressions. These figures are direct binomial-interval calculations based on your class sizes. citeturn14search4turn14search10

The small-sample validation literature reaches the same qualitative conclusion. In a classification sample-size study focused on scarce diagnostic data, Beleites et al. concluded that **75–100 independent test samples** are often needed just to test a good-but-not-perfect classifier with reasonable precision, and that with **5–25 independent samples per class**, random testing uncertainty can completely mask learning-curve behavior. Your minority classes are far below that regime. citeturn15search0turn15search9

On imbalance, the main limit is informational, not only algorithmic. Resampling, class weighting, and cost-sensitive learning can improve optimization and average metrics, but they **cannot create class support or true separation that is absent in the original data**. Surveys in imbalanced learning still treat resampling and cost-sensitive methods as standard mitigations, but the literature also emphasizes that **class overlap** is often the bigger problem, and oversampling near overlap regions can inject synthetic noise or unstable boundaries. That means the evidenced limit of mitigation is clear: if minority classes have only a handful of weakly separable, partly noisy cases, more model engineering or resampling will not reliably recover high recall. More and cleaner real data is the usual remedy. citeturn19search8turn14search3turn19search13turn19search11turn19search23

**Evidence and sources.** The quantitative precision statements come from binomial interval methodology and sample-size planning for classification. The imbalance conclusions come from recent imbalanced-learning surveys and overlap studies. citeturn14search4turn14search10turn15search0turn19search8turn14search3turn19search11

**Confidence.** **High** that minority-family accuracy and recall are not statistically supportable at single-digit \(N\); **medium** on any claim about the best achievable overall top-1 percentage, because that depends on separability and label trustworthiness in addition to sample size. citeturn15search0turn19search11
