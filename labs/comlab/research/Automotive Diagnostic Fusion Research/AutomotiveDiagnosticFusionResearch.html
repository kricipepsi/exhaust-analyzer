Multi-Source Evidence Fusion and Calibrated Probabilistic Networks for Legacy Petrol Engine Diagnostics

Executive summary

The diagnostic engine under study, which operates on legacy petrol passenger cars (model years ≈ 1990–2010), faces a diagnostic performance plateau with top-1 accuracy limited to . Analysis indicates that the current hand-tuned, additive, weighted "prune-then-rank" classifier is the primary system constraint. This linear, deterministic architecture is incapable of resolving non-linear diagnostic spaces, conditional sensor dependencies, or severe sensor contradictions.

A systematic review of cross-domain benchmarks reveals that hand-tuned rule bases suffer an absolute performance penalty of  compared to learned statistical classifiers and Bayesian networks1. To overcome this constraint without discarding valuable expert knowledge, a transition to a calibrated probabilistic fusion model is required. This report demonstrates that:

  1. Additive rule engines have an inherent mathematical ceiling that prevents scalable multi-class diagnostics under noisy or missing data conditions3.
  2. Sensor contradictions can be modeled as active diagnostic features using Dezert-Smarandache Theory (DSmT) or Proportional Conflict Redistribution (PCR5/6) to prevent Zadeh's paradox4.
  3. Conditional dependencies between live sensor data and exhaust chemistry must be resolved via Stacked Bayesian Networks or Log-Odds Aggregator frameworks to prevent "swamping" reliable channels6.

Rule-based vs learned rankers: the achievable ceiling

The first research question addresses the performance limit of hand-tuned rule-based classifiers relative to learned statistical rankers on multi-class diagnostic tasks with noisy, partially missing evidence.

Finding

Hand-tuned rule engines and additive weight rankers consistently plateau at top-1 accuracies of  on complex multi-class diagnostic tasks involving noisy or incomplete data3. Under identical data conditions, learned statistical and probabilistic classifiers—including Bayesian networks, Support Vector Machines (SVMs), and Random Forests—routinely achieve top-1 accuracies of 1. The absolute performance gap between hand-tuned rule systems and learned approaches ranges from  to over 1.

The primary ceiling for the current "prune-then-rank" engine is its linear additive structure. It cannot resolve non-linear diagnostic boundaries where symptoms overlap, nor can it handle cases where diagnostic trouble codes (DTCs) are absent, which starves the rule base of its primary triggers3.

Evidence and sources

The most rigorous head-to-head comparison between a hand-tuned rule-based expert system and a Bayesian network on identical noisy datasets is documented in the HEPAR medical diagnostic project3. HEPAR-RB is a hand-crafted rule-based expert system utilizing 178 hierarchical production rules3. HEPAR-BN (and its successor HEPAR II) is a causal Bayesian network containing over 70 nodes and conditional probability tables (CPTs) learned from clinical cases3. Both models were evaluated on the IFF dataset (699 patients) and the DH dataset (181 patients) to diagnose complex, overlapping disorders under conditions of missing symptoms and laboratory findings3.

High. The HEPAR benchmark represents a long-term, peer-reviewed evaluation of rule-based and Bayesian networks on identical, noisy real-world data3.

Data gaps and domain-transfer caveats

The HEPAR dataset represents a medical domain3. However, the domain transfer to automotive fault diagnosis is structurally valid. Medical findings (patient symptoms, physical signs, and blood tests) correspond to the four automotive diagnostic channels under study: operator symptoms, live sensor telemetry, 5-gas exhaust chemistry, and DTC presence.

Both systems map noisy, incomplete, and conditionally dependent features to a discrete, mutually exclusive or near-exclusive label space. Unlike human physiology, automotive systems are bounded by rigid thermodynamic and chemical equations (such as Stoichiometric combustion chemistry), which should make a probabilistic physical structure more robust than its medical counterpart.

Contradiction as a discriminator without destructive override

The second research question examines principled mathematical methods that allow a classifier to use evidence contradiction as an active diagnostic feature, rather than as a hard, destructive override.

Finding

When independent evidence channels contradict each other—such as when live ECU sensor data indicates a stoichiometric mixture () but the 5-gas exhaust analyzer measures high oxygen levels () indicating a lean burn—traditional rule-based systems rely on hard overrides. These overrides are highly sensitive to single-channel failures, often resulting in diagnostic errors.

In evidence theory, classical Dempster's rule of combination fails under high contradiction (), leading to overconfident, counter-intuitive conclusions known as Zadeh's paradox4.

To constructively utilize contradiction without destructive overrides, modern multi-sensor fusion architectures employ Proportional Conflict Redistribution (PCR5 or PCR6) rules4. These methods mathematically isolate the conflicting probability mass and redistribute it locally to the specific channels generating the contradiction, preserving the semantic meaning of the disagreement4.

Confidence

High. The mathematical limitations of classical DST and the robustness of PCR5/6 rules under high contradiction are well-established in multi-sensor fusion theory5.

Data gaps and domain-transfer caveats

While the mathematics of PCR5/PCR6 is highly mature, empirical implementation within automotive engine control units (ECUs) is extremely rare. Legacy vehicle diagnostics (1990–2010) are severely limited by computational constraints (8-bit to 32-bit microcontrollers with low memory limits). The localized nature of PCR calculations (which require storing cross-products of beliefs for combinations of hypotheses) represents a major structural barrier for on-vehicle embedded systems5. However, this is not a constraint for off-board PC-based garage diagnostic scan tools.

Calibrated-likelihood / Bayesian fusion vs additive weights

The third research question evaluates how calibrated-likelihood and Bayesian fusion approaches compare with simple additive weighting when evidence channels are conditionally dependent, and how to prevent weak channels from being swamped.

Finding

The naive Bayes conditional independence assumption—which assumes that the joint probability of the evidence is the product of individual channel probabilities—is highly destructive when channels are correlated19. In the engine under study, volumetric efficiency cross-checks, calculated engine load, and short/long-term fuel trims are highly correlated physical responses to a single root cause (e.g., an intake manifold vacuum leak).

Under naive independence assumptions, these correlated features cause "swamping," where the overlapping signals inflate the joint probability of a specific family, completely drowning out independent diagnostic channels (e.g., raw gas chemistry)19.

To prevent swamping while preserving diagnostic performance under missing-evidence states (such as the absence of a DTC), three architectural corrections are validated6:

  1. Bayesian Belief Networks (BBNs): Directed Acyclic Graphs (DAGs) that explicitly model the causal dependencies between sensors6.
  2. Stacked Generalization (Stacking over channels): Using logistic regression or a Log-Odds Precedent Aggregator (LOPA) to aggregate channel-level predictions, which naturally prunes redundant dependencies7.
  3. Leak Probabilities in Noisy-OR structures: Allocating a non-zero probability to unmodeled causes to prevent the network from collapsing to zero when evidence channels are starved20.

Evidence and sources

In complex engine systems like diesel and petrol powertrains, research shows that a pure model-based or data-driven diagnostic network often fails under variable loads or when sensors degrade21.

To address this, a "Belief Rule-Based" (BRB) expert system was developed for marine diesel engines, which integrates expert-elicited qualitative rules with numerical sensor data using Dempster-Shafer evidential reasoning22. This hybrid model was benchmarked against Artificial Neural Networks (ANN), Support Vector Machines (SVM), and binary logistic regression under five-fold cross-validation22:

  • Diagnostic Performance on Engine Wear: The optimized BRB model achieved a top-1 diagnostic accuracy of 24. It consistently outperformed the standalone ANN, SVM, and binary logistic regression models in terms of diagnostic stability, computational footprint, and multi-fault identification23.
  • Handling Starved Inputs: By incorporating a structural "leak probability" parameter—representing the background probability of unmodeled causes—the model maintained stable classifications even when input indicators were missing20.

Furthermore, in multi-DTC vehicle electronic systems, incorporating a causal BBN rather than standard decision trees dramatically reduced misdiagnoses6. Under standard rule-based structures, if any intermediate sensor in the logical chain fails or is absent, the system misdiagnoses or stalls6.

The BBN models the joint probability  and can reversely infer the most likely failure cause even when multiple DTCs are missing or logged simultaneously6.

To mitigate feature correlation and swamping, the Log-Odds Precedent Aggregator (LOPA) replaces standard naive Bayes product fusion with a log-odds aggregation of per-source reliability estimates7. LOPA mathematically prunes redundant chains and prevents highly correlated symptoms from compounding and overwhelming the independent diagnostic channels7.

Confidence

High. The mathematical consequences of conditional independence violations (such as the XOR limitation and joint probability inflation) are proven statistical realities19. BBN implementations in complex engine platforms show consistent performance gains6.

Data gaps and domain-transfer caveats

The high-performing BRB and BBN engine benchmarks cited are predominantly conducted on marine diesel powertrains22 or simulation models (e.g., CWRU rolling bearing databases)26. These systems exhibit slower dynamic transients than passenger car petrol engines, which must continuously adapt to fast-changing driver demands and closed-loop three-way catalyst behaviors.

Furthermore, parameterizing the CPTs of a large Bayesian Network requires highly specialized software tools (such as GeNIe or SMILE) and significant historical datasets27, which are rarely standardized across legacy automotive service networks.

Could not be found list

The following gaps in empirical evidence have been identified in the literature search:

  1. Direct Head-to-Head Comparative Benchmarks for Legacy Petrol Fleets: No publicly documented, peer-reviewed datasets exist that directly compare a hand-tuned additive rule-based engine against a Bayesian Network or Random Forest using the four exact diagnostic channels under study (5-gas exhaust chemistry, live OBD-II sensor streams, generic P-codes, and operator symptoms) for model years 1990–2010.
  2. Computational Benchmarks for PCR5/6 on Embedded Legacy ECUs: Quantitative data documenting the execution time and memory footprint of Proportional Conflict Redistribution rules (PCR5 or PCR6) compiled on 8-bit, 16-bit, or early 32-bit automotive microcontrollers (e.g., Motorola 68HC11 or Infineon TriCore) is absent.
  3. Statistical Characterization of Operator-Reported Symptom Noise: No peer-reviewed studies quantify the exact prior probability distributions, error rates, or likelihood noise for qualitative operator complaints (e.g., "runs rough") when fused with quantitative combustion chemistry datasets in real-time diagnostics.
