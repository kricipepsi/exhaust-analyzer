# Trouble Shooter

### **Too Much of a Good Thing**

_I’m working on a 2015 GMC Yukon SLT with a 5.3L V8 flex-fuel engine and over 100,000 miles on the odometer. The owner is complaining of poor idle quality, surge and poor fuel economy, and the MIL is on._

_I checked for stored DTCs and found rich fuel mixture codes for both banks. Driveability seemed okay during a road test, with no lack of power. I can reset the fuel trims to zero, but long-term fuel trim for both banks hits −35 in less than a minute after that. If I reset the fuel trims and erase the codes, then start the engine cold, fuel trims behave normally until the engine reaches operating temperature at idle. Then long-term fuel trim spikes to −35 in about 30 seconds. Blocking the purge line didn’t affect fuel trims, but removing the engine’s PCV vent tubes did._

_Upstream oxygen sensor readings are rich, but they aren’t fixed and the system appears to be in closed loop. The ECT, IAT, mass airflow and MAP readings all appear normal. Fuel pressure reads 45 psi at idle. The mass airflow reads 4 gps at 550 rpm, which seems low. Ethanol content is about 3%. I was unable to find any software updates or service bulletins in my service information._

_Jeff Gilbert_

_Atlanta, GA_

![2_TroubleShooter_0618](https://www.motor.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/2_TroubleShooter_0618.png)

When fuel trims trend negative, it’s because the engine is getting too much fuel and the PCM is trying to subtract fuel to return the mixture to stoichiometry. This usually occurs over a fairly narrow range, with the PCM constantly overshooting the mark, with the result being that the engine alternates between running slightly rich to slightly lean. This doesn’t seem terribly efficient, but there are reasons for this system. We won’t discuss them here because they aren’t germane to your current situation, Jeff.

When fuel trims spike in one direction, either negative or positive, it’s because the PCM is doing everything it can to try to alter the fuel mixture, and isn’t able to accomplish its goal. In your case, the engine is receiving a fuel mixture that’s very rich and the PCM is doing its best to change the situation. Its main tool is injector pulse width, which it will shorten as much as possible to limit the amount of fuel that’s injected into the engine each time the injectors open.

Because this is a gasoline direct injection (GDI) engine, the PCM also has control of the high-pressure fuel pump. Although the high-pressure pump is mechanically operated and driven by a lobe on the camshaft, the PCM can influence its delivered fuel pressure by adjusting the pulse width of a solenoid inside the pump. The 45 psi you measured is the fuel pressure of the low-pressure fuel pump. The high-pressure pump can deliver in excess of 3000 psi to the injectors, which can’t be measured with your conventional fuel pressure gauge.

You didn’t mention in your note whether you had checked the long-term and short-term fuel trims at any engine speeds other than at idle. Ideally, fuel trims should be checked at idle, at 1500 rpm and at 2500 rpm. Checking fuel trims at different speeds and when the engine is under load and unloaded will help us determine whether the PCM is able to maintain normal fuel trims under these varying conditions. Does the engine run rich only at idle, but clean up somewhat at higher engine speeds? Your customer had mentioned a surge, which you failed to detect during a road test. This might also have revealed itself as a sudden change or fluctuation in fuel trims.

Since the PCM is trying to subtract so much fuel from both banks on your customer’s engine at idle, it’s obvious that it’s getting too much fuel from a source that the PCM can’t control. What are some of the possible sources? The first that comes to mind is a leaking injector or injectors. The PCM can limit an injector’s pulse width but has no control over how much unwanted fuel passes through the injector if it’s stuck open or isn’t closing completely.

While it’s not impossible, it’s unlikely that there are leaking injectors on both banks. We’d expect to find a leaking injector on one bank or the other, in which case fuel trims would be affected on that bank alone. Your engine has extremely negative fuel trims on both banks at idle, so whatever is influencing fuel mixture is affecting both banks equally.

Next in line would be fuel pressure, which would affect both banks equally. The PCM has limited control over fuel pressure, so it’s possible that too much fuel would be injected into the cylinders each time the injectors opened, regardless of how brief the PCM tried to make the duration of each injector pulse width. There should be a PID for the high-pressure fuel pump, so you should be able to determine whether the PCM is successfully controlling the pump. Here again, the PCM will try to rein in fuel pressure to limit the amount of fuel that reaches the cylinders if its sensors are telling it that they’re already getting too much.

You mentioned that you had plugged the evaporative emissions system purge line. An intermittently sticking or stuck evap system purge valve is a prime suspect for rich fuel mixtures. If the purge valve is stuck open, it will allow fuel to be purged from the charcoal canister and dumped into the intake manifold at all times. This will be noticed most often at idle, as the extra fuel vapors from the evap system are more than the engine can use at that speed and load. At higher engine speeds and loads, any evap system vapors can be assimilated by the engine and the PCM can offset their presence by adjusting fuel trims. You’ve already ruled out the purge valve by blocking the hose and noting that fuel trims did not change.

One of the last things you mentioned provides the biggest clue. Disconnecting the engine’s vent tubes affected fuel trims. This is a positive indication that the engine is getting extra fuel from the crankcase, but how did it get there? If the engine were running very rich, we might expect to find fuel in the crankcase because it had been washed down the cylinder walls and past the piston rings. It would then be sucked back into the intake manifold, along with normal blow-by gases. In this case, though, there’s another cause.

GM has identified a problem with the high-pressure fuel pump on vehicles like your customer’s Yukon, as well as a number of other GM vehicles equipped with different versions of the same engine. GM says the high-pressure fuel pump may leak, resulting in a fuel odor (if it’s leaking externally) and/or increased evaporative emissions (if it’s leaking into the crankcase, as is the case with your customer’s vehicle). The engine may also fail to start or provide less power when driven, due to insufficient fuel pressure.

A recall was issued to address these symptoms. It’s described in GM Bulletin 14858, which was issued in 2015. Your customer should have received a notification of the recall by mail at that time. Instruct your customer to check with the dealer to determine whether they’re still conducting the repairs on recalled vehicles and whether his is still covered. If it is, he’ll thank you for pointing him in the right direction. If it’s not, he can return to your shop and you can replace the pump and the intermediate fuel feed pipe for him. Always exercise proper care when dealing with the high pressure in a GDI fuel system.
