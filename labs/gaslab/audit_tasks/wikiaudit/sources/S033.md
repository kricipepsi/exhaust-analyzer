# Cat Smarts

The catalytic converter sure has a lonely job; it rarely comes to mind unless it creates a driveability problem or emissions failure. I’m told that converter replacements are a tough sell in areas without an emissions program. But with more parts of the country falling into nonattainment due to tightened air quality standards, converter sales should become more commonplace.

OBD II systems also shift the converter equation. Think about it: Will a customer in a nonemissions area be comfortable driving with a constantly illuminated MIL when his converter fails? Once again, this spells new opportunity for shop owners.

Catalytic converters can be adversely affected by physical or thermal damage, or chemical poisoning. Physical damage is usually pretty straightforward to diagnose. Either the converter has obvious external damage or you can pound the cool converter with your fist to determine if the substrate (ceramic honeycomb) is loose.

Thermal damage is caused by overheating (usually from misfire) and comes in three stages. First is reduced function from the washcoat crystals (the noble elements coating the substrate) slightly melting and combining into larger crystals, which reduces the overall surface area. The second stage totally destroys the washcoat from too much heat. The final stage is total substrate meltdown, and is the most common cause of converter plugging.

There are three ways to test for a plugged converter/exhaust system. The first is to drop the exhaust and create a large leak in the system. This must always be done upstream from the possible point of restriction. On vehicles with the converter built into the manifold (such as Mitsubishis), you can remove the pulse air pipe to create additional exhaust airflow. Caution: Hot exhaust may be directed at flammable parts or the hood! I use a 1-inch-diameter piece of copper tubing bent to direct the exhaust toward the ground. Take the same precaution when removing exhaust piping to test for a restriction.

The second method of pinpointing an exhaust restriction is with a vacuum gauge. First, hook up your gauge, then test idle vacuum and record the reading. Next, rev the engine to 3000 rpm, hold it there, then record the reading again. Normally, the second reading will be higher. If it’s more than 2 inches less than the idle reading, suspect a plugged exhaust. Using a vacuum gauge is fast but not always accurate because it may not catch a partial clog (airflow is low with no load). Also, timing advance problems or flat cam lobes can mimic an exhaust restriction when you use a gauge, giving you false indications.

The final and probably most accurate method of finding an exhaust restriction is with a pressure tester. Remember, test upstream from the converter. Also, be mindful of the direction of exhaust flow to your test point. I once removed the AIR injection line from the rear of the cylinder head on a 5.8-liter Ford Van. The exhaust flow went straight into my test hose. On acceleration, I recorded pressures over 10 psi-definitely a cause for concern! Then I dropped the exhaust to see the difference in pressures. There was none! Whoops! The exhaust directed into the test point created a false indication of backpressure. You should be okay with 4 psi or less under full acceleration. By the way, oxygen sensor holes and AIR penetration points make excellent test points.

If you determine that a converter is plugged, there are two courses of action. You can remove the converter, hog it out and reinstall it. This is illegal in the U.S. and unprofessional as well, but still not uncommon in some areas. Or you can replace the converter with an approved unit in the same location.

Note that I said -the same location.- Regulations are very specific about this. The location of the converter has a lot to do with its light-up time and operating temperature. Close to the manifold means faster light-up and higher running temperatures, further back, the opposite. The OEMs do very specific testing for this, in addition to testing for heat gain of items close to the converter (brake lines, fuel lines, fuel tanks, floorboards, etc.). Moving the converter location or using a nonapproved converter can trigger a lawsuit.

Converters can be coated and poisoned by many sources and chemicals-lead and sulfur from gasoline, zinc and phosphorus from oil consumption, silicone from coolant and carbon from older, low-mileage driv-ers. Lighting off the converter and running the fuel system rich can reverse some sulfur and carbon contamination because it causes increased catalyst temperatures. Take care when doing this regeneration, however, to ensure that the converter doesn’t overheat. EPA papers also indicate that using low-sulfur fuel can reverse sulfur degradation somewhat.

So here are the million-dollar questions: When a car fails an emissions test, is it because the gases before the converter are too high for it to handle or because the converter is inefficient? And how much pollutants can a good converter handle before its long-term survival is threatened? I’ve never found the answers to these questions in any OE manual or SAE or EPA document.

The following examples should help guide you in the right direction. One caveat: This information is gleaned from in-use testing on specific vehicles (mainly my van), and may not relate to all vehicles.

The converter and engine were dead cold prior to the test (82/F-remember, I live in Arizona). Notice the high O2 readings? This is from the AIR injection system, which is diluting the other readings.

Chart 2 (below) is the same vehicle with the AIR injection system disconnected and plugged. If you want accurate exhaust readings, you must remove and plug all air injection lines. Simply disabling the pump or diverter valves is not enough; some air may leak past the closed valves. Note that the gas readings aren’t too bad and that the readings before and after the cat are the same. This is because the converter has not lit up yet.

So what do we know so far? Judging by the cold gas readings, this is a pretty decent-running engine. Note that a cold converter won’t affect the gas readings, so you can get a pretty good indication of what the converter has to handle without tapping in before the cat. Usually, letting a converter cool down for 20 minutes will allow enough time for this test even if the engine has been warmed up. The cold test is not a good indicator for NOX, however, because NOX must be tested under a load, which will warm the converter right away.

Chart 3 on page 36 is the same engine warmed up and in closed-loop. Now the converter is operating, as indicated by the different before and after readings. As you can see, the converter does not have to work very hard at cleaning up HC and CO on a well-running engine.

In Chart 4 (page 36), I drove the mixture rich by pinching off the vacuum signal to the MAP sensor. Notice that the converter is handling the high CO very nicely. Even its temperature is in bounds. Keep in mind that this testing was done at no-load, so the airflow wasn’t nearly as high as in normal driving.

Many older carbureted engines were designed to run at 2.0% CO at idle and cruise, allowing the converter to clean up the exhaust. Anything over that may damage a converter. Newer, well-running fuel injected engines should run under .5% CO preconverter in closed-loop, unless running some long-term idle strategy.

Keep in mind that long/rich idle strategies generally won’t damage a converter because of overall low air volume. Also, remember that the oxidation of CO to CO2 doesn’t release nearly as much heat as the conversion of HC to CO2 and H2O. Why? CO has one carbon atom to oxidize, while HC in gasoline is usually a large molecule like C6H16. The difference is in the quantity of atoms to be oxidized.

Chart 5 on the next page is indicative of the true converter killer-misfire. This is why OBD II is so concentrated on misfire; it’s the main cause of premature converter failure. Look at the temperature of the exhaust behind the converter on the bottom line of this chart. The 800/F reading was not a final temperature-we aborted the test here. With a shell temperature of 800/, the internal temperature of the converter had to be somewhere near 1200/. At 1400/F, the washcoat starts to get damaged. Remember, this was still a no-load condition. A misfire adds fuel and O2 to the converter. Note the increase in CO from the O2 sensor seeing the misfire and enriching the mixture to compensate for the perceived lean mixture. Remember, the O2 sensor measures only O2 levels, not fuel mixture!

If you have a marginal emissions failure and good fuel control (O2 sensor sweeping), test the converter cold to try to get an indication of preconverter gases. If the converter looks weak, create a misfire to burn off any possible contamination fouling the washcoat (again, use caution to avoid overheating the converter). Then retest. If the converter can’t generate high enough temperatures to control the emissions of a single-cylinder misfire, then suspect a weak converter.

So far we’ve discussed HC, CO and the oxidization portion of the catalyst. NOX emissions are controlled by the reduction portion of the catalyst in the front of the converter. NOX testing is completely different than for the other gases. First, the cold test is completely ineffective. So how do you differentiate between a true problem and an inefficient converter? This can really be answered only by preconverter and postconverter gas testing. To avoid misdiagnosis, however, be sure to eliminate possible excessive NOX causes in the engine before proceeding with preconverter testing. These include a lean mixture, too much timing advance, EGR problems, an overheated engine or intake, combustion chamber deposits and too much O2 in front of the cat.

HC and CO are generally low on a properly running engine, so the oxidation portion of the converter doesn’t have to work very hard. NOX is just the opposite; a good-running engine has a fairly high concentration of it-1800 to 2500 ppm. A good converter should be able to reduce that by about 2000 ppm. Suppose a vehicle is failing NOX at 2000 ppm. The key question then becomes: Is the converter dead or is the engine producing gobs of it-say, 4000 ppm? After eliminating the most likely causes, the only way to know for sure is to get into preconverter gas testing.

Note that the reduction portion of the converter will not work in the presence of too much oxygen. A properly running engine in closed-loop should have .5% to 1.0% O2 in the exhaust. Every .1% increase in O2 will decrease the catalyst’s ability to reduce NOX by about 100 ppm. At 3.0% O2 in the upstream exhaust, the reduction portion of the catalyst shuts down. Note that the reduction reaction actually needs some CO to do its thing. The ability of the converter to get CO for reduction and store O2 for oxidation depends on the swing of the fuel mixture back and forth past stoichiometry.

Cleaner-burning fuels, heated converters, heavily insulated converters (some will stay hot for 24 hours) and converter temperature sensing seem to be the future for emissions controls, according to SAE papers. But with greater demands for cleaner tailpipe emissions required now, the short-term future of present-day catalytic converters seems assured. And now you know how to test them!
