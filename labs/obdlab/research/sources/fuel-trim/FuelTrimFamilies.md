Step 1: Verify the Concern: The diagnostic process begins by confirming the customer's complaint. This involves verifying warning lights, checking driveability symptoms, and interviewing the driver to determine key environmental variables. Technicians document whether the issue occurs during cold start, acceleration, cruising, or prolonged idling.Step 2: Visual Inspection and Basic Tests: Statistics indicate that $10\%$ to $30\%$ of all engine performance issues can be identified through a rigorous visual inspection. This step includes checking for obvious faults like disconnected vacuum lines, split hoses, loose intake ducts, or corroded electrical connections. Technicians must also perform basic fluid assessments. For example, checking the oil dipstick with a match or lighter can reveal gasoline contamination if the oil flames up, while bubbling or boiling indicates coolant intrusion. The intake ductwork must be checked for obstructions, such as animal nests or debris, which restrict airflow.Step 3: Retrieve Diagnostic Trouble Codes and Scan Data: Technicians connect a scan tool to the OBD-II diagnostic link connector (DLC) to retrieve active, pending, or history codes, and capture freeze frame data to analyze engine conditions at the moment the fault occurred.Step 4: Preliminary Electrical Tests: This step requires verifying battery voltage under load, checking alternator charging voltage ($13.5\text{ V}$ to $15.0\text{ V}$ at $2000\text{ RPM}$), and testing primary chassis grounds to prevent false sensor readings.Step 5: Engine Performance Tests: Technicians evaluate overall engine health using vacuum gauges, fuel pressure gauges, cylinder compression testers, and exhaust gas analyzers to verify mechanical integrity before focusing on electrical components.Step 6: Test Components Related to Stored DTCs: Pinpoint testing is conducted on suspected sensors or actuators using digital multimeters, hand-held vacuum pumps, and lab oscilloscopes.Step 7: Recheck Diagnosis and Complete Repairs: After identifying the root cause, technicians replace or repair the faulty component to manufacturer specifications.Step 8: Road Test and Verify Fix: The final step involves road testing the vehicle under the exact freeze frame conditions to verify that the fault has been resolved and that the powertrain control module (PCM) can complete its self-test monitors.Evolution of Fuel Trim Architecture and Mathematical Control TheoryAutomotive emission regulations led to a transition from discrete numeric fuel monitoring to percentage-based closed-loop control systems.Under the OBD-I standard, GM fuel injection systems utilized two primary variables in Keep Alive Memory (KAM) :Integrator: Represented short-term real-time fuel corrections based on oxygen sensor feedback.Block Learn: Represented long-term learned fueling adaptations developed over operating time.The OBD-I data stream represented these values on a numeric scale of $0$ to $255$, centering at the neutral value of $128$. If the ECU detected a lean air-fuel ratio, it added injector pulse width, raising the Integrator or Block Learn value between $129$ and $255$. Conversely, to correct a rich mixture, it subtracted fuel, dropping values between $0$ and $127$.The implementation of OBD-II standardized these variables as Short Term Fuel Trim (STFT) and Long Term Fuel Trim (LTFT), using a percentage scale from $-100\%$ to $+100\%$. A value of $0\%$ represents a neutral state where no correction is added to the base injector pulse width equation. The mathematical transition between these standards is defined by the following linear equation :$$\text{Fuel Trim}_{\%} = \left( \frac{\text{Numeric Value} - 128}{128} \right) \times 100\%$$In modern closed-loop control, the PCM calculates injector pulse width ($T_{\text{inj}}$) using input data from the oxygen sensor, intake air temperature, engine load, and engine speed. The relationship between STFT and LTFT is sequential; LTFT acts as a learned variable that tracks and absorbs long-term trends from STFT to return the short-term value to $0\%$.If the combined correction ($\text{STFT} + \text{LTFT}$) exceeds pre-programmed adaptive thresholds (typically $\pm 20\%$ to $\pm 25\%$), the PCM illuminates the Malfunction Indicator Lamp (MIL) and stores a DTC. Under Wide Open Throttle (WOT) or heavy acceleration, the PCM suspends closed-loop feedback and locks STFT at $0\%$ to prevent learned corrections from lean-skewing the enrichment strategy.

The PCM relies on three distinct temporal memory structures to store fault data :Active DTC: Set when a monitor fails during a single drive cycle (or two consecutive cycles for two-trip codes). This is recorded in freeze frame data, preserving a snapshot of engine parameters at the moment of failure.History DTC: If the system is repaired or the fault is no longer detected, the PCM will turn off the dashboard MIL after three consecutive driving trips without a failure. However, the DTC remains stored in History memory.Automatic Erasure: The PCM automatically erases History DTCs and freeze frame data after forty consecutive warm-up cycles, provided no further emission failures are detected.To verify repairs efficiently, technicians can use "Field Service Mode" on supported GM platforms. Grounding Pin 6 of the OBD-I connector or the designated test terminal on the 16-pin connector while the engine is running activates this mode. This allows technicians to run diagnostics and observe real-time fuel trim responses without setting new DTCs during testing.Understanding these storage cycles is essential for diagnosing intermittent faults. Clearing codes before retrieving freeze frame data destroys valuable diagnostic context.
