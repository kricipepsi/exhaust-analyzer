The three most common internal mechanical faults that cause excessive oil consumption in petrol vehicles involve oil bypassing seals and entering the combustion chamber, where it burns off. [1, 2]
## 1. Worn or Damaged Piston Rings
Piston rings sit around the pistons to maintain a tight seal against the cylinder walls. They have two primary jobs: keeping combustion gases in the chamber and scraping oil off the cylinder walls. [3, 4, 5]

* The Fault: Over time, friction wears down the rings or the cylinder walls. Alternatively, carbon buildup can cause the rings to stick. [3, 4, 6]
* The Result: The gap widens, allowing engine oil to slip past the piston into the combustion chamber, where it burns alongside the petrol. [3, 4]
* Key Symptoms: Blue-grey smoke from the exhaust (especially under acceleration), fouled spark plugs, and a noticeable drop in engine compression or power. [1, 6, 7]

## 2. Degraded Valve Stem Seals
Valves sit at the top of the engine cylinder head to let air and fuel in, and exhaust gases out. [4, 5, 8]

* The Fault: Valve stem seals prevent oil lubricating the camshaft area from dripping down into the combustion zones. Because these seals are made of rubber or silicone, the high-operating temperatures of petrol engines cause them to harden, crack, or wear down over time. [4, 5, 9, 10, 11]
* The Result: Oil seeps down the valve guides directly into the intake or exhaust ports. [9, 12]
* Key Symptoms: A distinct cloud of blue smoke upon a cold start or after idling for a long period (e.g., waiting at a red light), which clears up as you continue driving. [4, 13, 14]

## 3. Faulty Positive Crankcase Ventilation (PCV) Valve [15, 16]
The [PCV system](https://www.tiresplus.com/blog/oil-change/why-is-my-car-burning-oil/) vents naturally occurring pressure and blow-by gases from the crankcase back into the engine intake to be safely re-burned. [4, 16]

* The Fault: The PCV valve can easily become clogged with oil sludge or get stuck in the open or closed position.
* The Result: If it sticks open, the high vacuum of the intake manifold forcefully sucks liquid oil vapor out of the crankcase and draws it directly into the engine to be burned. If it sticks closed, crankcase pressure builds up so high that it forces oil past other internal gaskets and seals.
* Key Symptoms: Rough idling, oil present inside the air intake boots/filter box, or unexpected external engine oil leaks. [4, 16, 17, 18, 19]

## 4. Blown Head Gasket
The head gasket is a critical seal sandwiched between the engine block and the cylinder head. It keeps combustion pressure, engine coolant, and pressurized motor oil isolated in their own channels.

* The Fault: Extreme engine overheating or physical degradation causes the gasket material to fail or warp. This creates a breach between an oil passage and a cylinder.
* The Result: High-pressure engine oil is forced straight into the combustion chamber during the intake stroke and burns away. It can also leak into the coolant passages, mixing oil and antifreeze.
* Key Symptoms: Thick white or blue-grey smoke from the exhaust, engine overheating, and a creamy, milky-brown sludge underneath the oil filler cap (often called "mayonnaise").

## 5. Worn Turbocharger Seals (For Turbocharged Petrol Engines)
Modern petrol engines increasingly rely on turbochargers to increase power. Turbochargers rotate at exceptionally high speeds and require a constant supply of pressurized engine oil for cooling and lubrication.

* The Fault: Dynamic rubber or metal seals inside the turbocharger assembly degrade due to extreme heat or contaminated engine oil.
* The Result: High-pressure oil leaks past the turbine seals directly into the intake tract (compressor side) or straight into the hot exhaust stream (turbine side), where it immediately vaporizes and burns.
* Key Symptoms: A significant drop in turbo boost/engine performance, heavy blue smoke during hard acceleration, and a thick coating of liquid oil inside the intercooler piping.
